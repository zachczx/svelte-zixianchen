Most of the useful things I know about interviews were told to me by somebody else.

Over the years, I picked up bits of advice about how to prepare, what to rehearse, what not to memorize, what to do with the questions I really hoped nobody would ask, and how to stop sounding like I was composing a written submission while speaking. Some of it made sense immediately. Some I ignored until I eventually learned the same lesson the hard way. Some only became clearer after I had sat on both sides of the interview table.

Enough of it stuck that this is now roughly how I prepare for interviews, and for the bigger presentations where I know the room may push back.

The thread running through almost all of it is simple: **make fewer things novel on the day**.

I am not trying to memorize the interview. I want the opening to feel familiar, the experiences I might use to be familiar, the uncomfortable questions to have crossed my mind before, and even the act of sitting there and thinking under pressure to feel slightly less new. Then I have more attention left for the part I cannot rehearse: actually listening to the people in front of me and responding to what they ask.

I am also assuming the basic homework here. Obviously understand the role, the organization and what it is dealing with. For a presentation, know your own deck. The more useful part to me is what happens after that.

I started writing about what makes interviews go well more generally, wandered very far away from the original question, and eventually split that into [another post about what I look for when I interview someone](/blog/what-i-look-for-in-interviews). This one stays with the practical advice I actually use.

And now it is yours.

## I say the answers out loud

The biggest thing for me is saying the answers out loud because it is very easy to sit somewhere thinking:

> Yes yes, I know how I will answer that.

Then somebody actually asks the question and somehow I spend four minutes explaining the background before getting anywhere near the answer that sounded so good inside my head.

I have caught this many times. Something that feels concise while I am thinking it becomes ridiculously long once I hear myself say it, and a beautiful sentence I wrote down can sound like a submission when it comes out of my mouth, because nobody talks like that.

So I talk to myself quite a lot before an interview. I will walk around the room saying an answer again, stop halfway because it sounds stupid, restart it, and eventually land on something that actually sounds like me.

### I remember the shape of the answer

I sometimes write an answer out first because writing helps me think. Once I start practicing, I care more about the rough shape and the experiences I might use. Sometimes there is also a line or two I like enough to remember.

Memorizing a whole paragraph falls apart as soon as the interviewer asks something slightly different, which they obviously will. Then I end up forcing a prepared answer into a question it does not fit.

The same experience can be useful in very different ways. A difficult project might come up when somebody asks about leadership, then the useful part may be the disagreement I had with someone, a mistake I made, the trade-off we chose, or what I regret and would do differently now. I want to know the experience well enough to talk about the part that actually answers what they asked.

There is such a thing as rehearsing too far too. If I can hear myself reciting the answer, I have probably crossed it. The goal is fluency, not giving the exact same performance every time.

### I save the mirror for the bigger ones

For most interviews, saying the answers aloud is enough for me. The mirror comes out when the interview feels especially consequential, or when I am rusty enough that I want to see what I actually look like while answering.

I did this particularly for my Public Service Leadership Programme (PSLP) application eons ago. There were a few interviews in that process, some with pretty big-shot panels, and I practiced those in front of a mirror a lot. It worked for me. By the time I got into the actual interview, I had already seen myself say the answers enough times that the whole thing felt much less foreign.

I can see it being useful when you are pretty new to interviews too, because you pick up your habits quickly. Once you know what you look and sound like, the mirror can go back to being a mirror.

## I record myself, unfortunately

Recording is another thing I bring out for the bigger interviews, and I hate watching myself on video, which is probably part of why this works so well.

You discover a lot of weird things you do without realizing it. I slouch and sometimes suck in through my nose for no reason when I am thinking. There are random nervous tics, shifting around in the chair, looking away at odd moments, and other stuff that feels totally normal while I am doing it and looks distracting when I watch it back.

Then there is all the verbal junk: "errrr", "erm", "basically", "actually", "well", and whatever else I apparently say while my brain catches up. I say some of these much more than I realize, and if every second answer starts with "well" or I keep saying "basically" while thinking, it gets very obvious on a recording. Basically, if I keep saying basically, then basically I have a problem.

Once I notice something, I can work on it. I sit a bit straighter, look more settled, and let myself pause for a second instead of immediately filling the silence with "errrr". A two-second pause feels much longer when you are the one being interviewed than it does to everybody else in the room.

I also try to stop doing the weird nose thing every time I need to think. I just want the distracting stuff to stop, preferably before half the panel spends the interview watching me suck air through my nose every time I need two seconds to think.

Recording also exposes how long the answers really are. If I thought I gave a quick response and the video says I have been talking for four minutes, okay, apparently it was not that quick.

## I rehearse the first few lines

The start of an interview is usually the point where I am most conscious that I am being interviewed. Once the conversation gets going, I tend to settle down and it feels much easier.

So I rehearse the first few lines more than the rest. I think about how I will introduce myself, what I might say if they start with some version of "tell us about yourself", and roughly how I want that first answer to begin.

The first minute gets most of my attention because I want it to feel familiar enough that my brain has one less thing to deal with while the nerves are still there.

## I mentally play through the whole interview

This part sounds slightly weird when I explain it, but I find it useful. I picture the whole thing beforehand, including the boring and awkward parts. I imagine arriving, walking into the room, greeting everybody, sitting down, hearing the first question, getting asked something difficult, pausing because I need to think, and eventually reaching the end and walking out again.

If it is online, I do the same with joining the call, watching everyone appear on screen and waiting for somebody to start.

I think of it as the nerdier office-worker version of what athletes do when they visualize a competition. The whole event feels a little more familiar when it actually happens, which helps because there is already enough going on in my head without also processing every part of the experience as new.

This is probably the purest version of making fewer things novel on the day. I cannot know what the panel will ask. I can at least make the setting itself less unfamiliar.

## I rehearse screwing up too

The mental run-through is not always a perfect interview where I answer everything beautifully and leave to imaginary applause. I think about what happens when something goes wrong too.

Maybe somebody asks something I genuinely did not anticipate. Maybe I start an answer and realize halfway through that I have gone down the wrong path. Maybe I know I could have answered the previous question much better. Maybe my mind just goes completely blank. Presentations have their own versions: I lose my place, somebody challenges an assumption I had not considered, the discussion goes somewhere strange, or a slide that seemed obvious at home suddenly needs much more explanation in the room.

I think it is worth preparing for those moments because the first mistake is often much less damaging than what we do after it.

If my mind goes blank, I can pause. If I do not know, I can say I have not dealt with that exact situation and reason from what I do know. If I realize an answer is going nowhere, I can stop digging, reset and answer the actual question. If I think later that I answered something badly, I can correct it briefly if a natural opening appears instead of spending the next ten minutes desperately trying to manufacture one.

The important part is to keep moving.

You already shat the bed. Do not spend the rest of the interview rolling around in it.

I do not want to announce that I am nervous, apologize three times, visibly replay the mistake in my head, or let one lousy answer contaminate the next five. Quite often the panel has already moved on. In a presentation, the room may barely register a stumble that feels enormous to me.

Recovery is part of the performance too. I cannot prepare for every thing that might go wrong, but I can prepare not to fall apart when one of them does.

## Come on, some questions are predictable

There will always be questions I did not expect, but interviews have enough predictable bits that I can think through the obvious ones beforehand. You can normally anticipate quite a lot from the role, your own experience and what the organization is dealing with.

For a leadership interview, I might think through questions like:

- Why do I want this role?
- What experience do I have that is particularly relevant?
- What difficult situation have I dealt with, and what did I actually do?
- What have I got wrong before?
- How do I deal with disagreement, especially with somebody senior?
- What does my leadership style look like in practice?
- What is likely to be difficult about this role?
- What would I do differently if I encountered a similar situation again?

The panel can phrase these any way they want. I just do not want the interview to be the first time I have seriously thought about something I could have predicted days earlier.

## I prepare the questions I hope nobody asks

This is probably the more useful part of the preparation because if there is a weak part of my experience, something that went badly, an awkward decision I made or an obvious question hanging over a project, I would rather wrestle with it at home than discover my answer while several people are staring at me.

I do the same thing with presentations. I look at the deck and ask where my evidence is weakest, which recommendation is likely to annoy someone, whether I have quietly made an assumption that somebody senior will spot immediately, and which slide I am praying nobody asks the obvious follow-up about.

That last one is usually a pretty good sign that I should prepare for the question.

It is quite comforting when the difficult question eventually appears and I realize I have already fought with some version of it before. I may still need to think because the question will rarely be identical, but at least I am not starting from zero.

## I know what I hope they remember

The panel controls the questions, and I still go in knowing the things about me or my experience that I hope they walk away understanding. I have definitely left conversations before and thought:

> Shit, we never talked about that at all.

So now I have a few things in my head that I hope surface somewhere. I still answer the question they actually asked, and if there is a natural opening to bring in an experience that matters, I use it.

I do the same with big presentations. If everybody forgets most of the deck by tomorrow morning, which they probably will, I should at least know what I hope survives.

## Some things only real interviews can teach

This is one piece of advice I wish I had followed more consistently: do more real interviews before the one you desperately care about.

I would not interview for random jobs I would never take just to practice. But there is something to be said for going for roles I could genuinely see myself taking, even if they are not the one I have already built up in my head as The Important One.

I do not interview often, so an important interview can also become the day I am shaking off the rust, which is not ideal.

All the rehearsal helps, but it does not reproduce the weirdness of sitting opposite people who are actually deciding whether they want you. Real interviews have awkward pauses, questions you misunderstood, a panel member whose face gives you absolutely nothing, and answers that wander somewhere you did not expect before you have to find your way back.

Those reps matter. I am writing this part as advice to myself too.

## I prepare the final question too

There is one question I can almost guarantee will appear in some form:

> Do you have any questions for us?

I always want something ready. It should be something I actually care about, because otherwise the question feels performative. I also avoid anything I could have answered by reading the organization's website. The questions I like most tend to get the panel talking about what the job is really going to be like.

One I like is:

> What do you think will be the hardest problem for the person coming into this role?

I genuinely want to hear the answer because it tells me something about what I might be walking into. There is also a nice side effect because sometimes somebody turns it around and asks me what I think, or how I would approach the problem, which is basically a free extra interview question on something I already thought about before I entered the room.

Yes, I absolutely think about this beforehand because it is an interview, of course I do.

### Know when to stop

Sometimes the panel answers my question, thanks me and starts wrapping up, so I let the interview end there.

Please do not respond with:

> Oh actually, can I tell you what I think about that?

If there is a natural opening in the conversation, I will engage with it and see where it goes. If everybody is obviously trying to end the interview, adding a bonus five-minute sales pitch is probably not going to save anything.

## Preparation helps me sound more like myself

Someone once asked whether I remain my "true authentic self" during interviews or put on a different image. I think I am still myself, obviously with a stronger filter than when I am having lunch with my friends. I know I am being assessed, so I choose my words more carefully and suppress some of the stupid shit I might otherwise say.

My views stay mine though. If I have a strong view about something relevant, I would rather explain what I actually think and why. Whoever hires me will meet the real version eventually, and I also have to work there, so successfully hiding everything inconvenient about me for an hour does not seem particularly useful to either side.

Preparation actually helps with this. The first version of an honest answer can be rubbish because I ramble, bury the point under five caveats, give way too much background or take forever to work out how I want to say it. Practicing means I have already gone through some of that mess before the interview.

Once I am comfortable with my own material, I also have more attention left for the people in front of me. I can listen properly, shorten an answer when they clearly get it, go deeper when somebody seems interested, and change direction when the question is not what I expected.

That is why I do not see preparation and authenticity as opposites. Good preparation should make me more responsive, not turn me into a script.

## Big presentations are the same problem with a different shape

I prepare for important presentations in much the same way, especially the ones where I expect disagreement after the deck ends.

The slides themselves worry me less because I control them and know what comes next. The discussion is where everybody else gets to take the conversation somewhere I did not plan, so I spend quite a lot of time preparing for that part too.

I practice the presentation aloud and listen for the bits that feel strange to say. I think about who is likely to disagree, where the argument feels weakest, which assumption could get attacked, and what question I really hope nobody asks.

I also want to be clear about what I actually need from the room. What decision am I asking people to make? Who is likely to hesitate? What are they likely to care about? I can deliver the deck perfectly and still fail if I answer the question I wanted to discuss instead of the one the room actually needs resolved.

Sometimes the hardest part is simply saying the difficult sentence in the room. Imagine people have spent years supporting an existing approach and my slide effectively says:

> We recommend stopping the existing approach because the evidence no longer supports it.

That sentence looks perfectly reasonable sitting here in a blog post. It feels quite different when there is history behind the decision, people have invested themselves in it and everybody is now looking at me.

I would rather hear myself say that sentence twenty times at home before the first real attempt happens in that room.

## I probably still overprepare

For the interviews I really care about, I probably do overprepare. I talk to myself a lot, and if the stakes feel high enough I may bring out the mirror or record myself too. I mentally run through the session, prepare the obvious questions, hunt for the uncomfortable ones and think about what I am going to ask at the end.

The work I have done over the years gives me the actual things to talk about. This preparation helps me get those things out of my head and into the room without spending half the interview on nerves, filler words or a four-minute background story that should have taken one.

After that, if they do not want me, so be it, because at least they got a fair look.

None of this is a secret system I invented. Most of it reached me in pieces over the years, as advice from other people, things I watched people do well, or lessons that finally made sense after I had ignored them once or twice. I kept the bits that worked for me, changed some of them, and eventually they became my own routine.

Someone else passed them to me. Now they are yours.
