At the ASEANext Forum 2026, Edwin Tong spoke about why working in the office still matters, especially for younger employees.

His example was the stupid question.

In an office, a junior employee can walk over to someone’s cubicle and ask one. Putting the same question into an email feels more formal and permanent, so people hesitate.

As he put it, it is very hard to learn when you cannot ask a stupid question.

I agree. I think the larger problem is that someone new often does not even know there is a question to ask.

## The hidden curriculum of staff work

People often think of staff work as writing papers, preparing slides or putting up submissions. Those are only its most visible products.

The real work includes understanding what decision is being sought, noticing what has been left unsaid, working out who needs to be consulted and anticipating what could go wrong.

Experienced officers may do these things almost automatically. A new officer sees the task written in the email.

That gap is difficult to teach because newcomers do not know what they are missing. They cannot ask about a consideration they have never encountered.

Sometimes the lesson comes through a red comment. Sometimes a colleague glances at the work and asks why Finance has not been consulted. Someone mentions that a similar attempt failed two years ago. A boss pauses over one sentence and asks a question that changes the framing.

The officer slowly learns what matters.

None of this resembles a formal course. It happens in fragments.

## Staff work is more than papers

Staff work also means doing a recce for an event and knowing what to look out for.

The tasking may cover the venue, seating, programme, audiovisual equipment and catering. It will not contain every cultural or operational consideration that could arise.

I remember an event involving Muslim guests where the Tiger Beer taps at the venue were covered so that everyone would feel more comfortable.

That sounds like a small detail. It is also exactly the kind of detail someone has to notice before a guest is placed in the awkward position of raising it.

Nobody is going to write an exhaustive email listing every such consideration. An experienced officer notices because someone pointed it out before, because they watched another officer work, or because they have learned to enter a venue and see it through the eyes of the people attending.

Staff work also includes learning to deal with bosses in person.

You get asked a question you did not anticipate. You become flustered. You give a poor answer. You get pressed further and realise the first question was pointing to a deeper concern.

Eventually, you learn to listen more carefully. You answer what was actually asked. You admit what you do not know. You stay composed when the discussion moves somewhere unexpected.

A carefully drafted email reply after half an hour does not teach this. Neither does composing a polished Teams message after consulting a few colleagues.

Some things have to be experienced live.

## Teams carries what people remember to say

Remote work makes this kind of learning harder.

A Teams meeting usually has a stated purpose. People enter, discuss the task, settle the immediate issues and leave. The senior officer explains what comes to mind.

Many assumptions remain unstated because they have become second nature.

The meeting may be perfectly competent. The new officer can still leave without knowing what was omitted.

Email is even more demanding. Seriously, who has time to write down every consideration for every task, for every officer, every time?

Even if someone tried, staff work is not a checklist that can be read from the top each morning. The considerations change with the issue, the audience and the decision being sought.

Email and Teams are good at transmitting explicit instructions. They are less reliable for passing down the things an experienced officer no longer realises need explaining.

Teaching also becomes private. One manager explains the same lesson separately to several officers across separate calls. Each explanation disappears when the call ends.

## The office was an accidental training system

Going to the office every day never guaranteed that someone would become good at staff work.

Some officers happened to sit near generous colleagues who explained their thinking. Others received “pls amend” and had to reverse-engineer what the boss wanted.

Plenty of people spent years in the office without becoming especially sharp.

Still, proximity did some work.

Knowledge travelled sideways. Officers overheard how another team handled a problem. Someone could glance at a draft before it became too developed. A junior could watch an experienced officer handle an uncomfortable exchange.

We called this learning by osmosis because there was no proper system.

Remote work removes much of that accidental contact. Scheduled meetings do not fully recreate it because the value often came from encounters that nobody thought to schedule.

## More staffing did not mean more wisdom

I do not want to romanticise the past.

Staffing used to be heavier. Papers went through more rounds. Officers consulted more people and prepared answers to more possible questions.

The volume of work was certainly higher.

It was never clear that everyone involved became smarter or sharper as a result.

Some officers developed excellent judgment. Others became good at satisfying the process. A long paper could still contain weak thinking. More clearance rounds sometimes improved the work. They could also compensate for the fact that the real issue had never been framed properly.

This is not a generational story. People learn what their organisations reward.

In the past, bosses often demonstrated seriousness by demanding more staffing. More pages and more rounds of clearance showed that a matter had been thoroughly considered.

The style being rewarded now is different.

## The low-fuss boss

There is a broad and understandable desire to reduce excessive staff work.

Nobody wants officers spending days polishing material that will have no effect on the decision. A boss who says, “Keep this short, don’t overwork it,” may save a team from a lot of pointless effort.

Being empathetic, pragmatic and low-fuss has also become a way of showing one’s chops as a leader.

That can be completely sincere. It is also a useful leadership tactic.

It tells staff that the boss trusts them, understands their workload and is not trapped by old bureaucracy. People appreciate it. It can score points in feedback and appraisal.

I am not judging this. I am calling it as I see it.

The older boss could signal competence by demanding more work. The modern boss can signal competence by waving work away.

Neither action tells us whether the eventual decision is any better.

The difficult part is that useful discipline and empty ceremony can look quite similar from a distance. Cutting both feels efficient until something important is missed.

Empathy, simplification and lower standards may also look identical in the short term.

## Why I have held the line

This tension appears in the way work-in-office policies are administered too.

Our policy requires officers to return to the office three times a week on predetermined days.

I am flexible about start and end times. What I have generally not allowed are swaps and partial-day deviations.

Someone may have a cohesion activity in the afternoon and ask to work from home that morning. Someone else may have returned for a meeting on a scheduled work-from-home day and ask to swap out another office day that week.

Each request sounds reasonable when viewed on its own. I still tend to say no.

I do not enjoy policing this. I also hate it when my own work-from-home day gets burnt because I have to attend a meeting in the office. I grumble and go in.

The policy does not treat that extra trip as a credit I can claim later.

Once we start doing that, the arrangement becomes an accounting system. An extra trip earns a day back. An afternoon activity justifies a morning at home. Every exception creates a comparison with the next one.

That is unfair to officers who quietly follow the arrangement without trying to renegotiate it. It also puts the manager in the position of deciding whose inconvenience is deserving enough.

More importantly, predetermined days have a collective purpose. They are supposed to create overlap.

If everyone can rearrange them around individual circumstances, we may still achieve three days of attendance on paper while losing the common presence that was meant to matter.

Our directors have cautioned officers against being too transactional about work-from-home days. I agree with that.

Coming into the office on an additional day does not create a debt that the organisation must repay. Work from home is still work. It is not leave or time off in lieu.

At the same time, transactional behaviour is a predictable response to a rule expressed as a number of days.

We say office attendance is about culture, relationships and informal learning. Then we express the requirement as three predetermined days and ask people not to treat those days as credits.

Someone still has to administer the rule consistently.

Holding the line may make me appear inflexible. Creating an unofficial system of swaps carries a different risk. It tells officers that the stated arrangement is only an opening position and that individual negotiation determines the real outcome.

I would rather be flexible within the policy than quietly replace it with one of my own.

## Attendance alone is not enough

This does not mean that requiring three office days automatically creates the workplace Edwin Tong described.

The junior employee must actually be able to approach someone. The colleague has to be present and willing to help. Teams need enough overlap for relationships to form. Senior officers need to explain their reasoning instead of transmitting only the final instruction.

An office full of people wearing headphones and joining separate Teams meetings may satisfy the policy. It does little for apprenticeship.

The harder question is what people are expected to gain from being there.

If the answer is learning and relationships, office time needs to be useful for those things. Otherwise, staff will understandably experience attendance as a commute imposed to meet a quota.

## Can the genie go back into the bottle?

Hybrid work has changed expectations. So has the move towards lighter staff work and more empathetic management.

I benefit from these changes too.

Together, however, they weaken some of the old ways professional craft was passed down.

The office provided incidental exposure. Heavy staffing produced comments and repeated practice, even when the process itself was excessive. Demanding bosses forced officers to anticipate questions, sometimes far beyond what the issue deserved.

The old model bundled waste and apprenticeship together.

We have been cutting the waste. Some of the apprenticeship may have gone with it.

It is unclear whether the genie can be put back into the bottle.

Once staff have been taught that good management means being low-fuss, renewed demands for rigour can look like regression. Once work-from-home days become part of normal working life, additional office attendance feels like something has been taken away.

Once older staffing practices have been labelled bureaucratic, it also becomes harder to explain why selected parts still matter.

Restoring the entire old system would be a mistake. It created too much work and did not reliably produce wisdom.

We still need something to replace what it carried.

## Why I keep writing about staff work

This is part of why I keep writing about staff work, and why I have been building Apptitude.

A website cannot teach judgment by itself. It cannot reproduce a good boss reviewing a real piece of work, or the experience of being pressed in a room and having to respond.

It can make more of the hidden curriculum visible.

It can show officers what to look out for, the mistakes that keep recurring and the questions that tend to arise. It can explain the reasoning behind common red comments. It can help someone recognise that a task involves more than producing the requested document.

Most importantly, it can tell a new officer that there is something here to learn.

That has to happen before the officer can ask the stupid question.

I sometimes wonder who has time to write all this down. The alternative is teaching the same lesson privately and inconsistently, one officer at a time, assuming anyone remembers to teach it at all.

Every old staff-work ritual does not deserve preservation. The judgment does.

The office once carried some of that judgment by accident. Heavy staffing carried some too. We have weakened both, often for valid reasons.

Counting office days will not replace them. Neither will nostalgia.

The next system has to teach the craft more deliberately, while making office time useful enough for the questions to emerge.

The worst gap is the invisible one: nobody knows there was a question.
